Why this exists

I spent a month in Peru, working in communities from Lima to Arequipa to Cuzco. Building roads and houses, digging trenches, clearing paths, helping put up schools. That trip was not about water, and I am not going to pretend it was. But it changed what I paid attention to when I got home.

I grew up in Arizona assuming that you turn on the tap and water comes out, and that this is simply how it works here. That is not true for everyone. On January 1, 2023, Rio Verde Foothills lost the hauled water that hundreds of homes depended on, after a dispute with Scottsdale. Some of those households still do not have a reliable, affordable way to get water. That is less than an hour from where I live.

The information that would have helped them already existed. It was just scattered across five or six government systems that do not talk to each other, in formats built for GIS analysts rather than for someone whose tank is empty. And the most basic question of all, where can I actually go get water right now, had no official answer anywhere. People found haulers the way people always find things when the system does not help: word of mouth, Facebook groups, and a website neighbors built themselves out of necessity.

So this starts with that question and answers it first. Where to get water, then who supplies you and whether that water has been safe, then the regulatory picture underneath it.

Arizona's water strain has many causes, and reasonable people disagree about how much each one matters. This is not trying to settle that. It is trying to make sure that whatever is true about your water, you can find it out now, instead of the way Rio Verde Foothills found out, after the water was already gone.

Sidak Mann, Arizona
